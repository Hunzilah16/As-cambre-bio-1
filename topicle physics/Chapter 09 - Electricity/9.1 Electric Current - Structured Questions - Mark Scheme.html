Part (b) [2 marks]

A charge of 60 × 10–6 C flows through a given section of a conductor in 140 × 10–3 s. Calculate the electric current, stating the final answer in mA.

Mark Scheme (2 marks):

To calculate current from charge and time:

  • Time, t  = 140 × 10–3 s

Recall the equation for current, I :

  • I = Qt{"language":"en","fontFamily":"Times New Roman","fontSize":"18","autoformat":true}

Substitute the numbers into the equation:

  • I = 60 × 10–6 140 × 10–3 {"language":"en","fontFamily":"Times New Roman","fontSize":"18"} = 4.286 × 10−4 A [1 mark]

[Total: 2 marks]

The charges in these questions are usually small fractions of a coulomb, so convert to standard form before substituting. In this question it was done for you, but in the exams, always do this right at the start, as it's very easy to forget later on.

Also remember to round all answers to the same significant figures as those used in the question.

Part (d) [3 marks]

When a copper wire is exposed to a potential difference, a current is detected in it.

Explain, with reference to charge carriers and electric fields, why there is a current detected in the wire.

Mark Scheme (3 marks):

Explain why a current is present in the wire:

Refer to the existence of free electrons in copper:

  • Copper wire has (many) free/conducting/valence/delocalised electrons; [1 mark]

Describe the effect of the potential difference:

  • The potential difference produces an electric field (in the wire); [1 mark]

  • (An applied electric field will make these) electrons accelerate (which is detected as a current); [1 mark]

[Total: 3 marks]

Copper is a good conductor of electricity, which means it has many free electrons. When placed in an electric field, or a region where there is a difference in electric potential, a force is applied to these free electrons causing them to accelerate.

Remember to convert all quantities into their standard units! Prefixes, like the ‘milliamps (mA)’ used in this question, are common tricks used by examiners to check if students are on their toes: in this question, we have to remember to convert 0.3 mA to A, as well as minutes to seconds, before substituting into the equation for current. You should know that conventional current flows from the positive terminal of a battery to the negative terminal – i.e., it is the flow of positive charge (even though, in reality, electrons flow around the circuit!)
